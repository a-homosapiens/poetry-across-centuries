# Modern & Classic Western Poetry: A 9th Grade Curriculum

**Overview:** A 9-module thematic journey tracing the evolution of Western poetry from Antiquity through the late 20th century. Each module pairs a foundational classic with a modern or contemporary response to explore how structure, tone, and perspective evolved over time.

---

## Module 1: Mythology Remixed (The Epic vs. The Personal)
*Why start here: 9th graders often have a strong baseline in Greek mythology. This serves as an engaging, familiar hook that immediately shows how modern poets flip classic stories to give voice to the voiceless.*
*   **The Classic:** Homer – Excerpt from *The Odyssey* (Ancient Greek, approx. 8th Century BC)
*   **The Contemporary:** Margaret Atwood – *Siren Song* (Canadian, 1974)

## Module 2: The Self and the Soul (The Expansive vs. The Internal)
*Introducing the two absolute titans of 19th-century American poetry. We look at how two poets living at the exact same time built completely opposite structures to define the self.*
*   **The Expansive:** Walt Whitman – *Song of Myself* (Excerpt) (American, 1855)
*   **The Internal:** Emily Dickinson – *I'm Nobody! Who are you?* (American, 1891)

## Module 3: The Natural World (Healing vs. Alienation)
*We look at how the Romantic view of nature as a spiritual cure was replaced by the Modernist view of nature as a reflection of psychological entrapment.*
*   **The Classic:** William Wordsworth – *I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud* (English, 1807)
*   **The Modern:** Rainer Maria Rilke – *The Panther* (German/Austrian, 1902)

## Module 4: Love and Anti-Romance (The Ideal vs. The Real)
*This tracks the evolution of the love poem, exploring how poets use—and deliberately break—the traditional sonnet form to strip away clichés and find a more honest version of romance.*
*   **The Classic:** William Shakespeare – *Sonnet 130* ("My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun") (English, 1609)
*   **The Contemporary:** Carol Ann Duffy – *Valentine* (Scottish, 1993)

## Module 5: The Cityscape and the New Rhythm (Poverty vs. Jazz)
*Here, we interleave the Harlem Renaissance. We contrast an early, highly structured critique of urban English poverty with a poem that fundamentally reshaped Western verse by injecting it with the syncopated rhythms of jazz and blues.*
*   **The Classic:** William Blake – *London* (English, 1794)
*   **The Modern:** Langston Hughes – *The Weary Blues* (American, 1925)

## Module 6: The Reality of Conflict (Heroism vs. Horror)
*Now that the student understands rhythm and structure, she can clearly see how industrial warfare forced poets to completely destroy the classical rhythm of the "hero."*
*   **The Classic:** Alfred, Lord Tennyson – *The Charge of the Light Brigade* (English, 1854)
*   **The Modern:** Wilfred Owen – *Dulce et Decorum Est* (English, 1920)

## Module 7: High Modernism and the Surreal (Fragmented Worlds)
*We introduce the dense, fragmented reality of post-WWI literature and the dark, dreamlike logic of the Spanish surrealists.*
*   **High Modernism:** T.S. Eliot – *The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock* (Excerpt) (American/English, 1915) 
*   **The Surreal:** Federico García Lorca – *Romance Sonámbulo* (Sleepwalking Ballad - Excerpt) (Spanish, 1928)

## Module 8: Ruins, Time, and Legacy (Empires vs. The Everyday)
*Contrasting the grand, sweeping view of history and dictatorial legacy with the quiet, absurd survival of domestic objects.*
*   **The Classic:** Percy Bysshe Shelley – *Ozymandias* (English, 1818)
*   **The Contemporary:** Wisława Szymborska – *Museum* (Polish, Late 20th Century)

## Module 9: Confronting Mortality (Defiance vs. Acceptance)
*For the final module, we look at how different eras structured their ultimate defiance against death, comparing tight, religious logic with roaring, emotional free-verse.*
*   **The Classic:** John Donne – *Death, be not proud* (English, 1633)
*   **The Modern:** Dylan Thomas – *Do not go gentle into that good night* (Welsh, 1951)
