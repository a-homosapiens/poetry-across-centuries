VI.  The Reality of Conflict

Glory, and the Lie of Glory

The Charge of the Light Brigade · Tennyson · 1854 ⟶ Dulce et Decorum Est · Owen · 1920
The Conversation

Two poems about war, written sixty years apart. One turns a catastrophic, doomed cavalry charge into something that sounds glorious and brave. The other takes you into a gas attack in the trenches and refuses to let it sound like anything but horror.

As you read, listen first — before you decide what each poem means, notice what it sounds like. Tennyson wants a drumbeat. Owen wants you to stumble.

Teacher’s Edition · History & Background

Sixty years, and the death of a heroic idiom

Alfred, Lord Tennyson was Britain’s Poet Laureate — the official national poet. He wrote “The Charge of the Light Brigade” within weeks of reading a Times report of the Battle of Balaclava (25 October 1854) during the Crimean War. He was never there. The charge itself was a disaster born of a garbled order: roughly 600 lightly-armed cavalry were sent straight up a valley into Russian artillery. Many were killed or wounded. Tennyson does not hide the blunder — “Someone had blundered” — yet he channels a driving, galloping meter to make the soldiers’ blind obedience sound noble and heroic. This is a public, consoling poem, written for a grieving nation.

Sixty years later, Wilfred Owen actually fought in the trenches of the First World War. Industrial engineering and chemical weapons had fundamentally changed what war was. Owen came to believe that Tennyson’s rhythmic, heroic style was itself a lie — that it flattered readers and recruited the next generation of the dead. “Dulce et Decorum Est” is a deliberate attack on that old classical ideal, built from the jagged, bodily imagery of a gas attack to argue that modern reality demanded a new, far more brutal kind of poetry.

Context worth having ready

Owen wrote the poem in 1917–18 while recovering from shell shock at Craiglockhart hospital, where he met and was mentored by Siegfried Sassoon. He was killed in action on 4 November 1918 — one week before the Armistice — and the poem was published posthumously in 1920. The title comes from the Roman poet Horace: “Dulce et decorum est pro patria mori” — “it is sweet and fitting to die for one’s country.” Owen calls it “the old Lie.” An early draft was pointedly addressed to Jessie Pope, a popular writer of patriotic recruiting verse.

The Poems · Read in Sequence

Classic · 1854

The Charge of the Light Brigade

Alfred, Lord Tennyson

(Excerpt)

Half a league, half a league,
Half a league onward,
All in the valley of Death
Rode the six hundred.

“Forward, the Light Brigade!
Charge for the guns!” he said:
Into the valley of Death
Rode the six hundred.

“Forward, the Light Brigade!”
Was there a man dismayed?
Not though the soldier knew
Someone had blundered.
Theirs not to make reply,
Theirs not to reason why,
Theirs but to do and die.
Into the valley of Death
Rode the six hundred.

Cannon to right of them,
Cannon to left of them,
Cannon in front of them
Volleyed and thundered;
Stormed at with shot and shell,
Boldly they rode and well,
Into the jaws of Death,
Into the mouth of Hell
Rode the six hundred.

❦ Sixty years later, from inside the trenches

Modern · 1920

Dulce et Decorum Est

Wilfred Owen

Bent double, like old beggars under sacks,
Knock-kneed, coughing like hags, we cursed through sludge,
Till on the haunting flares we turned our backs,
And towards our distant rest began to trudge.
Men marched asleep. Many had lost their boots,
But limped on, blood-shod. All went lame; all blind;
Drunk with fatigue; deaf even to the hoots
Of gas-shells dropping softly behind.

Gas! GAS! Quick, boys!—An ecstasy of fumbling
Fitting the clumsy helmets just in time,
But someone still was yelling out and stumbling
And flound’ring like a man in fire or lime.—
Dim through the misty panes and thick green light,
As under a green sea, I saw him drowning.

In all my dreams before my helpless sight,
He plunges at me, guttering, choking, drowning.

If in some smothering dreams, you too could pace
Behind the wagon that we flung him in,
My friend, you would not tell with such high zest
To children ardent for some desperate glory,
The old Lie: Dulce et decorum est
Pro patria mori.

Close Reading

Read, then look closer

Work through these on your own or with a partner. Point to the exact words that make you say what you say.

  • Read the first three lines of each poem aloud. How does each one make your voice move? Where does Tennyson speed up? Where does Owen make you slow down or stop?
  • Tennyson admits “Someone had blundered.” Why do the soldiers charge anyway? What does the poem seem to admire about them?
  • Owen fills his poem with the body — boots, blood, lungs, a drowning man. Find three of these. Why do the physical details matter to his argument?
  • Owen ends by speaking to “My friend.” Who do you think he is talking to — and what does he want them to stop doing?
  • Both poems could be handed to a teenager thinking about going to war. Which one is trying to inspire them? Which one is trying to warn them? How can you tell?

The heart of the pairing

The form is the argument. Tennyson uses rhythm to make death feel noble; Owen breaks that rhythm to show you what it actually costs.

How to run the close reading

Lead with the ear, then the eye

1. Sound before sense. Read both openings aloud (or have students do it) before any discussion of meaning. Tennyson’s “Half a league, half a league” is dactylic — a stressed syllable followed by two light ones — and it gallops. Have students clap or tap it; the meter enacts the charge and builds a momentum that sweeps doubt aside. Then read Owen’s “Bent double, like old beggars under sacks”: the heavy, clustered stresses and mid-line pauses make the reader trudge. The contrast is the whole lesson in miniature.

2. Track the diction. Build two columns on the board. Tennyson trades in abstraction and nobility — “valley of Death,” “the six hundred,” “the jaws of Death,” “Boldly.” Owen trades in the body and the concrete — “blood-shod,” “coughing like hags,” “guttering, choking, drowning.” Ask: which words would each poet refuse to use?

3. Follow the point of view. Tennyson stays at a reverent distance, third person, plural and anonymous (“the six hundred”). Owen moves from “we” to a single witnessed “him” (“I saw him drowning”), then turns and points at “you.” Grief becomes personal and then accusatory.

4. Name the turn. Owen’s poem pivots at “In all my dreams” — the attack invades the speaker’s sleep — and again at “If… you too could pace,” where the poem drops its scene and addresses the reader directly. That final direct address is where the argument lands.

Tennyson’s move

Acknowledge the blunder, then override it with rhythm and duty. Obedience itself becomes the heroism (“Theirs not to reason why”).

Owen’s counter-move

Refuse consolation. Slow the reader down, force them to watch one man die, then name the comforting slogan as “the old Lie.”

Guiding questions · with what to listen for
  1. Where, exactly, does Tennyson admit something went wrong — and how does the poem keep going anyway? Listen for “Someone had blundered” landing in a single quiet line, immediately buried under “Theirs not to reason why.” The poem converts a mistake into a test of loyalty. Push: does admitting the blunder make the praise more honest, or more manipulative?
  2. Owen ends mid-argument, speaking to “My friend.” Who is “you”? Listen for students realizing “you” is the reader — and specifically anyone who repeats patriotic slogans to “children ardent for some desperate glory.” (Historical hook: an early draft named Jessie Pope, a recruiting-verse poet.)
  3. Whose soldiers have faces? Whose have names? Listen for the recognition that neither poem names an individual — but Owen gives us one body in unbearable close-up, while Tennyson keeps “the six hundred” as a single glorious mass. Individuation vs. monument.
  4. The Latin tag means “it is sweet and fitting to die for one’s country.” Why does Owen quote it in another language, and in small caps, at the very end? Listen for the idea that the borrowed classical authority is exactly what he’s dismantling — he lets the grand phrase arrive, then flattens it by calling it “the old Lie.”
  5. If meter is a kind of persuasion, what is each poem persuading you to feel? Listen for the payoff: Tennyson’s regular gallop persuades you death here is meaningful; Owen’s broken rhythm and gagging sounds persuade you it is waste. Form is argument.
Annotation prompts · hand these to students

On the Tennyson

Underline every repetition (“half a league,” “Cannon to…,” “Rode the six hundred”). In the margin, mark whether each repetition feels like a drumbeat, a prayer, or a chant — and why the poem might want that effect.

On the Owen

Circle every word tied to the body or the senses. Draw an arrow at each point where the rhythm trips or stops (dashes, caesuras, the short two-line stanza). Where the poem breaks, ask: what is breaking — the line, the soldier, or the reader’s comfort?

The thesis of the pairing

Between 1854 and 1918 the machinery of war changed, and so poetry’s duty changed with it. Tennyson could still use form to console; Owen weaponizes form to indict, insisting that a war of gas and industry could no longer be sung in a galloping meter without lying.

Words & Allusions
league
An old unit of distance, roughly three miles. “Half a league” measures the ground still to charge.
Light Brigade
A unit of light cavalry — fast, lightly armed horsemen, not meant to charge heavy artillery head-on.
Poet Laureate
Britain’s official national poet. The title helps explain Tennyson’s public, patriotic stance.
blood-shod
Owen’s coinage: soldiers “shod” (shoed) in their own blood after losing their boots.
lime
Quicklime, a caustic chemical that burns flesh — the horror of “a man in fire or lime.”
guttering
Like a candle flame sputtering out; here, a man’s life failing.
Dulce et decorum est
pro patria mori
Latin, from the Roman poet Horace: “It is sweet and fitting to die for one’s country.” Owen names it “the old Lie.”
dactyl
A metrical foot — one stressed then two unstressed beats (HALF a league). The galloping engine of Tennyson’s poem.
