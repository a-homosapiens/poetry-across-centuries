IV.  Love and Anti-Romance

The Onion, Not the Rose

Sonnet 130 · Shakespeare · 1609 ⟶ Valentine · Duffy · 1993

Love poems are full of clichés: eyes like the sun, lips like coral, red roses, heart-shaped cards. Both of these poets are sick of them — and both write a love poem that throws the clichés out to find something more honest.

Shakespeare does it with a straight face and a wink: his mistress is nothing like the sun, and he loves her anyway. Nearly four hundred years later, Duffy does it with a vegetable: instead of a rose, she hands her lover an onion.

Teacher’s Edition · History & Background

Clearing away the fake roses

Shakespeare’s Sonnet 130 (published 1609) is a comic anti-love-poem — a parody of the flowery convention, inherited from the Italian poet Petrarch, of praising a beloved by comparing her to the sun, coral, snow, roses, and goddesses. Shakespeare walks through the whole cliché checklist and denies every item: her eyes are “nothing like the sun,” her breath “reeks,” she “treads on the ground” like an ordinary human being. Then the closing couplet turns warm: he swears his love is “as rare / As any she belied with false compare.” The joke has a serious point — real love doesn’t need lies.

Carol Ann Duffy’s “Valentine” (1993) picks up the same fight in a modern voice. Duffy — later the UK’s Poet Laureate (2009–2019) — rejects the usual greeting-card emblems of love, the roses and heart-shaped cards, and offers instead an onion. Across the poem the onion becomes a startlingly truthful symbol: it makes you cry, its scent clings to your hands, its rings can stand for a wedding ring — and, mishandled, it can wound. Where Shakespeare’s honesty is affectionate, Duffy’s pushes into love’s difficulty, possessiveness, and danger.

Context worth having ready

The convention both poets attack is the blazon: an itemized catalogue of the beloved’s features through flattering comparison. Shakespeare subverts it from inside the sonnet — he keeps the fourteen lines and the couplet but fills them with anti-flattery. Duffy abandons fixed form altogether, using free verse with short, blunt lines — at one point shrinking to a single stark word meaning deadly — to sound like a real, slightly dangerous person speaking. “Valentine” comes from her 1993 collection Mean Time.

The sonnet’s rules, in full

This is the first strict classical form students meet in its original, untranslated English, so it’s worth spelling out completely. A sonnet is fourteen lines, always. Shakespeare’s English (or “Shakespearean”) sonnet arranges those fourteen lines as three quatrains (four-line units) followed by a closing couplet (a rhymed pair), rhymed ABAB CDCD EFEF GG — each letter a new rhyme sound, so the whole poem only ever rhymes in alternating pairs until the final couplet locks it shut. Every line is written in iambic pentameter: ten syllables, five “iambs” of an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed one, close to the natural rise and fall of English speech (“my MIS-tress’ EYES are NOTH-ing LIKE the SUN”). The three quatrains typically build an idea across roughly equal steps, and the couplet delivers a volta, a turn, that reframes everything before it — exactly what happens here at “And yet, by heaven…” This is the form students will meet again, in variant shapes, in Pairings VIII and IX.

The Poems · Read in Sequence

Classic · 1609

Sonnet 130

William Shakespeare

“My mistress’ eyes are nothing like the sun”

My mistress’ eyes are nothing like the sun;
Coral is far more red than her lips’ red;
If snow be white, why then her breasts are dun;
If hairs be wires, black wires grow on her head.
I have seen roses damasked, red and white,
But no such roses see I in her cheeks;
And in some perfumes is there more delight
Than in the breath that from my mistress reeks.
I love to hear her speak, yet well I know
That music hath a far more pleasing sound;
I grant I never saw a goddess go;
My mistress, when she walks, treads on the ground.
And yet, by heaven, I think my love as rare
As any she belied with false compare.

What happens in the poem

Duffy’s speaker refuses the usual Valentine’s Day gifts — roses, heart-shaped cards — and instead hands her lover an ordinary onion. Across the poem she turns that onion into a strikingly honest symbol of love. It makes your eyes water the way real love can; its scent clings stubbornly to your hands; its pale, papery skin and concentric rings suggest both a moon and a wedding ring; and, handled wrongly, it can sting and even wound. What begins as a joke slowly becomes a serious — and slightly dangerous — picture of what loving someone actually involves.

Close Reading

Read, then look closer

Work through these on your own or with a partner. Point to the exact words that make you say what you say.

  • Make a list of the clichés Shakespeare denies (sun, coral, snow, roses, perfume, goddess). Why spend twelve lines saying what his mistress is not?
  • Read Shakespeare’s last two lines. How does the whole poem suddenly turn from insult to love?
  • Why an onion? List everything an onion does (makes you cry, clings, has rings, has layers). How is each one secretly true about love?
  • At one point Duffy’s poem shrinks to a single, blunt one-word line meaning deadly. What does it mean that a love poem can turn dangerous?
  • Both poets reject fake romance. Whose version of “real love” feels more honest to you — Shakespeare’s warm ordinariness, or Duffy’s fierce onion?

The heart of the pairing

Both poems throw out the roses of cliché to find something truer. Shakespeare keeps the sonnet and loves a real, ground-treading woman; Duffy keeps nothing but honesty, and hands you an onion that can make you cry — and cut.

How to run the close reading

Two poets refusing the same lie

1. Name the target: the blazon. Establish the convention both poems attack — the flattering catalogue that compares a beloved to the sun, coral, roses. Shakespeare recites the checklist and denies each item; the humor comes from how bluntly he does it (“the breath that from my mistress reeks”).

2. Find the volta in the couplet. For twelve lines the sonnet reads like an insult. Then “And yet, by heaven…” flips everything: his love is genuinely rare precisely because he refuses to lie about her. Have students paraphrase the couplet in their own words — the whole poem hinges on it.

3. Track Duffy’s extended metaphor. “Valentine” is built on a single conceit: love is an onion. Walk the transformations — the onion looks like a pale moon under its papery skin (beautiful), it stings the eyes to tears (love hurts), its scent clings to the hands (love marks you), its rings can stand for a wedding ring (commitment), and mishandled it can wound (love can possess and harm). One humble object carries the poem’s whole argument.

4. Compare the two honesties. Shakespeare’s realism is affectionate — the mockery clears the air so real tenderness can land. Duffy’s is unnerving — she insists real love also stings, lingers, and can turn dangerous. Ask which honesty students find braver, and why.

Shakespeare’s method

Subvert the sonnet from within — keep the strict form, fill it with anti-flattery, then redeem it all with a loving couplet. Wit in service of tenderness.

Duffy’s method

Abandon the form — free verse, blunt lines, a startling everyday object — to make love feel raw, real, and a little threatening.

Guiding questions · with what to listen for
  1. Why does Shakespeare keep the sonnet form while mocking the sonnet’s usual content? Listen for the productive tension: the elegant, traditional container makes his plain-spoken honesty land harder. He’s reforming the love poem from the inside, not smashing it.
  2. Duffy could have chosen any object. Why is an onion a better symbol for love than a rose? Listen for the multiple truths an onion carries — tears, layers, a lingering scent, concentric rings, the power to sting. A rose only flatters; an onion tells the truth about love’s difficulty.
  3. What changes when Duffy shrinks a whole line to one blunt word meaning deadly? Listen for how the isolation makes the word detonate. The one-word line enacts a threat and marks the poem’s darkest turn — love as something that can possess and harm.
  4. Shakespeare’s poem ends warmly; Duffy’s ends uneasily. What does each ending say about what love is? Listen for the contrast: for Shakespeare, love is clear-eyed devotion to a real person; for Duffy, love is powerful, faithful, and potentially dangerous — not safe, but true.
  5. Are these anti-love poems, or the most honest love poems of all? Listen for the recognition that rejecting cliché is itself an act of love — both poets care enough to refuse the easy, false version.
Annotation prompts · hand these to students

On the Shakespeare

Underline every comparison he denies. Draw a line between line 12 and line 13 — the volta. In the margin, rewrite the final couplet as a text message. Does the meaning survive?

On the Duffy

Every time the onion becomes something new, jot the “real” meaning beside it (tears → love hurts; rings → marriage). Circle the poem’s darkest word. At what point does the gift start to feel like a warning?

The thesis of the pairing

Nearly four centuries apart, both poets clear away the counterfeit roses of love poetry. Shakespeare does it with a wink — keeping the sonnet and loving a flawed, human woman; Duffy does it with an onion — discarding the form and insisting that real love also stings, clings, and can wound. Honesty, in both, turns out to be the deepest romance.

Words & Allusions
sonnet
A 14-line poem in a fixed form. Shakespeare’s type ends with a rhymed two-line couplet that often delivers a twist.
blazon
A conventional love-poem device cataloguing the beloved’s features through flattering comparisons — the tradition both poets mock.
Petrarchan
In the style of Petrarch, the Italian poet whose idealized love sonnets set the clichés Shakespeare parodies.
conceit / extended metaphor
A single comparison developed at length through a whole poem — Duffy’s onion.
volta
The “turn” in a sonnet where the argument shifts — here, Shakespeare’s final couplet.
dun
A dull greyish-brown. “Her breasts are dun” deliberately refuses the cliché of snow-white skin.
damasked
Patterned or variegated (of a rose), mixing red and white — a prized, decorative bloom.
Poet Laureate
A nation’s official poet. Duffy held the British post from 2009 to 2019, the first woman to do so.
