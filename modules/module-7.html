VII.  The Visionary Fragment & the Surreal

Fragments and Sleepwalkers

Kubla Khan · Coleridge · 1797 ⟶ Romance Sonámbulo · Lorca · 1928

Both of these poems refuse to “make sense” the old way — but for opposite reasons, and a century apart. Coleridge claims his poem came to him whole in an opium dream, then broke off unfinished when a visitor interrupted him: what’s left is a shimmering, half-glimpsed vision of a pleasure-dome that was never meant to resolve into a plot. Lorca throws logic out entirely and writes like a dream, drenched in the color green.

Don’t panic if you can’t summarize either one in a sentence. That difficulty is the point — and it’s worth learning how to read a poem you can feel before you can fully explain it.

Teacher’s Edition · History & Background

Two ways to break the story

Samuel Taylor Coleridge’s “Kubla Khan” (composed 1797, published 1816) is one of English Romanticism’s strangest and most hypnotic poems. Coleridge claimed it came to him complete in an opium-influenced dream, inspired by a passage about the Mongol emperor Kubla Khan’s palace at Xanadu — and that he woke and began transcribing it, only to be interrupted by “a person from Porlock” on business; when he returned to the desk, the rest had vanished. He published it with the subtitle “A Vision in a Dream: A Fragment,” and its broken-off, unfinished quality became inseparable from its spell. The poem is not fragmented like a shattered mind — it is fragmented like an interrupted dream, cut off mid-vision.

Federico García Lorca’s “Romance Sonámbulo” (“Sleepwalking Ballad,” 1928, from Romancero gitano / Gypsy Ballads) breaks the story a different way. It takes the old Spanish romance (folk-ballad) form and fills it with the dream-logic of surrealism. A green girl waits on a balcony; a wounded man climbs toward her through the moonlit night; there is blood, longing, and death — but the narrative stays elliptical and hypnotic, ruled by its incantatory refrain, “Verde que te quiero verde” (“Green, how I want you green”). You feel the poem before you can decode it.

Context worth having ready

Both poems trade the smooth, logical story for something closer to trance — but from a century apart and in different keys. Coleridge’s poem is a visionary fragment: a sublime, half-remembered dream-landscape that resists narrative because it was, by Coleridge’s own account, literally cut short. Lorca’s is a fully-composed surrealist ballad: image, color, and music replace cause-and-effect by design, not by accident. Students read Coleridge in his own English; Lorca appears here in the public-domain Spanish original with a plain English translation prepared for this curriculum, so the class can hear the music of the original refrain.

The Poems · Read in Sequence

Romantic · 1797

Kubla Khan

Samuel Taylor Coleridge

Complete text (public domain)

In Xanadu did Kubla Khan
A stately pleasure-dome decree:
Where Alph, the sacred river, ran
Through caverns measureless to man
  Down to a sunless sea.
So twice five miles of fertile ground
With walls and towers were girdled round:
And there were gardens bright with sinuous rills,
Where blossomed many an incense-bearing tree;
And here were forests ancient as the hills,
Enfolding sunny spots of greenery.

But oh! that deep romantic chasm which slanted
Down the green hill athwart a cedarn cover!
A savage place! as holy and enchanted
As e’er beneath a waning moon was haunted
By woman wailing for her demon-lover!
And from this chasm, with ceaseless turmoil seething,
As if this earth in fast thick pants were breathing,
A mighty fountain momently was forced:
Amid whose swift half-intermitted burst
Huge fragments vaulted like rebounding hail,
Or chaffy grain beneath the thresher’s flail:
And ’mid these dancing rocks at once and ever
It flung up momently the sacred river.
Five miles meandering with a mazy motion
Through wood and dale the sacred river ran,
Then reached the caverns measureless to man,
And sank in tumult to a lifeless ocean:
And ’mid this tumult Kubla heard from far
Ancestral voices prophesying war!

The shadow of the dome of pleasure
Floated midway on the waves;
Where was heard the mingled measure
From the fountain and the caves.
It was a miracle of rare device,
A sunny pleasure-dome with caves of ice!

A damsel with a dulcimer
In a vision once I saw:
It was an Abyssinian maid,
And on her dulcimer she played,
Singing of Mount Abora.
Could I revive within me
Her symphony and song,
To such a deep delight ’twould win me,
That with music loud and long,
I would build that dome in air,
That sunny dome! those caves of ice!
And all who heard should see them there,
And all should cry, Beware! Beware!
His flashing eyes, his floating hair!
Weave a circle round him thrice,
And close your eyes with holy dread,
For he on honey-dew hath fed,
And drunk the milk of Paradise.

Modern · 1928

Romance Sonámbulo

Federico García Lorca

Opening — Spanish original (public domain)

Verde que te quiero verde.
Verde viento. Verdes ramas.
El barco sobre la mar
y el caballo en la montaña.
Con la sombra en la cintura
ella sueña en su baranda,
verde carne, pelo verde,
con ojos de fría plata.
Verde que te quiero verde.

A plain English translation, prepared for this curriculum

Green, how I want you green.
Green wind. Green branches.
The ship upon the sea
and the horse on the mountain.
With shadow at her waist
she dreams on her balcony,
green flesh, green hair,
with eyes of cold silver.
Green, how I want you green.

Close Reading

Read, then look closer

Work through these on your own or with a partner. Point to the exact words that make you say what you say.

  • Coleridge fills Xanadu with contradictions: a “sunny pleasure-dome” with “caves of ice,” a “holy and enchanted” chasm that is also a “savage place.” Why might a dream-vision be full of things that shouldn’t belong together?
  • The poem simply stops after the vision of the Abyssinian maid and her song — there’s no ending, no resolution. Coleridge called it “a fragment.” Does the poem feel unfinished to you, or does it feel complete on its own terms?
  • The final lines describe a poet with “flashing eyes” and “floating hair” who has “fed” on “honey-dew” and “the milk of Paradise” — and other people react with fear and awe. What is Coleridge suggesting about what it costs, or what it means, to have visions like this one?
  • Read Lorca’s opening aloud in Spanish, even if you don’t speak it. What do you feel from the sound and the repeated word “verde” (green), before you know what it means?
  • Lorca makes wind, branches, flesh, and hair all green. Green isn’t logical here — it’s a feeling. What might all that green stand for: life? sickness? longing? death?

The heart of the pairing

Both poems trade the tidy story for something closer to a trance. Coleridge offers a sublime vision interrupted mid-dream; Lorca dissolves reality into the hypnotic color and dream-logic of the surreal. One you follow like an unfinished dream; the other you follow like a completed one.

How to run the close reading

Teaching poems that resist paraphrase

1. Give students permission not to “solve” them. Both poems reward feeling and attention before full comprehension. Say so up front. The skill this module builds is tolerating difficulty — reading for image, sound, and mood, and letting meaning assemble slowly.

2. Tell the poem’s own origin story first. Share Coleridge’s account: the whole vision came to him in an opium dream after reading about Kubla Khan’s palace; he woke and began writing it down; a visitor on business (“a person from Porlock”) interrupted him for over an hour; when he returned to the desk, the rest of the vision had dissolved. Whether or not students believe the story, it explains why the poem behaves the way it does — a landscape assembled from vivid, disconnected fragments, then simply cut off.

3. Let Lorca’s refrain do its work. Read “Verde que te quiero verde” aloud in Spanish and let the class hear the incantation before translation. Then ask what “green” is doing: it saturates wind, branches, skin, and hair, refusing to settle into one meaning. This is surrealism — image and color carrying emotional truth that plain statement can’t.

4. Name the two kinds of dream-logic. Draw the contrast explicitly. Coleridge’s poem behaves like a dream because it claims to be one, transcribed and then broken off — the sublime, the sacred, and the savage sit side by side without needing to reconcile. Lorca’s poem behaves like a dream by design: a finished work of art built from surrealist technique. Both ask readers to follow image and feeling rather than plot.

Coleridge’s Romantic vision

A sublime dream-landscape, offered as literally interrupted and unfinished. Meaning arrives through awe, contradiction, and the sound of the verse.

Lorca’s surrealism

The liberated dream, fully composed: color, music, and image replace cause and effect. A folk ballad becomes a hypnotic vision you feel more than follow.

Guiding questions · with what to listen for
  1. Why does Coleridge call the poem “a fragment” rather than finishing it? Listen for the idea that the interruption is part of the poem’s meaning — a vision this intense and strange can’t simply be picked back up and completed to order. The brokenness becomes evidence of the vision’s power.
  2. What effect does it have that the “sacred river” runs through “caverns measureless to man” into a “sunless sea”? Listen for a landscape built at a scale beyond ordinary human measurement — the sublime, where beauty and vastness edge into something closer to fear.
  3. In Lorca, does it matter exactly what “green” means? Listen for the recognition that green resonates precisely because it won’t resolve — suggesting youth, nature, sickness, jealousy, death all at once. Surreal images hold several meanings in suspension.
  4. How does the Spanish sound — the repetition, the vowels — shape the poem’s spell? Listen for the ballad’s music: the returning refrain and rolling rhythm make it feel chanted or dreamed, so meaning arrives through the ear.
  5. Both poems leave you without a clear resolution. Is that a failure, or the point? Listen for the payoff: Coleridge’s poem stages an interrupted vision and Lorca’s an unresolved dream — both are honest pictures of an experience that never promised to add up neatly in the first place.
Annotation prompts · hand these to students

On the Coleridge

Circle every place two opposite things sit side by side (sunny/caves of ice, holy/savage, pleasure/war). Underline anything that feels vast or immeasurable. Where does the poem shift from describing Xanadu to describing the poet himself — and what changes?

On the Lorca

Highlight every appearance of “verde” / green and everything it colors. In the margin, jot the feeling each green thing gives you. Don’t translate the poem into a plot — map its moods instead.

The thesis of the pairing

A century apart, both poets abandon the smooth, orderly story for something closer to a trance. Coleridge offers a sublime vision he insists arrived whole and left him mid-sentence; Lorca surrenders logic altogether to the color, music, and dream-image of the surreal — an interrupted vision beside a fully dreamed one.

Words & Allusions
Romanticism
The late-18th/early-19th-century movement that prized imagination, emotion, nature, and the visionary over reason and rigid form.
the sublime
Beauty mixed with awe or terror — a landscape or vision so vast or intense it overwhelms ordinary understanding, as in Xanadu’s “caverns measureless to man.”
“a person from Porlock”
The unnamed visitor Coleridge blamed for interrupting him mid-transcription, after which the rest of his opium-dream vision allegedly vanished.
Xanadu
The legendary summer capital of the Mongol emperor Kubla Khan, which Coleridge transforms into a dreamlike pleasure-garden.
surrealism
An art movement embracing dream-logic, the unconscious, and startling irrational images — the mode of Lorca’s ballad.
romance
Here, the traditional Spanish narrative ballad form Lorca borrows and transforms — not a love story. Its rule: lines of eight syllables each, with the same assonant (vowel-only) rhyme running through every even-numbered line, while the odd-numbered lines rhyme with nothing at all. That loose, half-rhymed net is part of why the poem feels chanted rather than end-stopped.
refrain
A repeated line — “Verde que te quiero verde” — that gives Lorca’s poem its hypnotic, song-like spell.
Romancero gitano
Lorca’s 1928 book Gypsy Ballads, rooted in the folk culture and landscape of Andalusia in southern Spain.
