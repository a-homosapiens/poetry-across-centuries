VII.  High Modernism & the Surreal

Fragments and Sleepwalkers

The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock · Eliot · 1915 ⟶ Romance Sonámbulo · Lorca · 1928
The Conversation

By the early 20th century, poets stopped telling smooth, logical stories. Both of these poems refuse to “make sense” the old way — but for opposite reasons. Eliot maps the anxious, fragmented inside of a nervous modern man who can never quite ask his question. Lorca throws logic out entirely and writes like a dream, drenched in the color green.

Don’t panic if you can’t summarize either one in a sentence. That difficulty is the point — and it’s worth learning how to read a poem you can feel before you can fully explain it.

Teacher’s Edition · History & Background

Two ways to break the story

T. S. Eliot’s “The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock” (1915) is a founding poem of High Modernism. It is an interior monologue — we are inside the head of Prufrock, a timid, over-educated, middle-aged man wandering a grimy modern city, working up the nerve to ask an “overwhelming question” (a declaration of love? a confrontation with the meaning of his life?) that he never manages to voice. The poem is fragmented, allusive, and ironic, full of unforgettable images — the evening “etherized upon a table,” a life measured out “with coffee spoons,” mermaids who will not sing to him. Its form mirrors a modern mind that cannot cohere or act.

Federico García Lorca’s “Romance Sonámbulo” (“Sleepwalking Ballad,” 1928, from Romancero gitano / Gypsy Ballads) breaks the story a different way. It takes the old Spanish romance (folk-ballad) form and fills it with the dream-logic of surrealism. A green girl waits on a balcony; a wounded man climbs toward her through the moonlit night; there is blood, longing, and death — but the narrative stays elliptical and hypnotic, ruled by its incantatory refrain, “Verde que te quiero verde” (“Green, how I want you green”). You feel the poem before you can decode it.

Context worth having ready

Both poets are dismantling the smooth Victorian narrative — but from opposite directions. Eliot fragments the intellect: irony, allusion, and self-cancelling hesitation portray a paralyzed modern consciousness. Lorca liberates the unconscious: image, color, and music replace cause-and-effect, so the poem behaves like a dream. Students read Eliot in his own English; Lorca appears here in the public-domain Spanish original with a plain English translation prepared for this curriculum, so the class can hear the music of the original refrain.

The Poems · Read in Sequence

Modern · 1915

The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock

T. S. Eliot

Opening lines, with selected later fragments

Let us go then, you and I,
When the evening is spread out against the sky
Like a patient etherized upon a table;
Let us go, through certain half-deserted streets,
The muttering retreats
Of restless nights in one-night cheap hotels
And sawdust restaurants with oyster-shells:
Streets that follow like a tedious argument
Of insidious intent
To lead you to an overwhelming question . . .
Oh, do not ask, “What is it?”
Let us go and make our visit.

In the room the women come and go
Talking of Michelangelo.

    —  later in the poem  —

For I have known them all already, known them all:—
Have known the evenings, mornings, afternoons,
I have measured out my life with coffee spoons;

And I have known the eyes already, known them all—
… And how should I presume?

I have seen the moment of my greatness flicker,
And I have seen the eternal Footman hold my coat, and snicker,
And in short, I was afraid.

I grow old . . . I grow old . . .
I shall wear the bottoms of my trousers rolled.
… Do I dare to eat a peach?

I have heard the mermaids singing, each to each.
I do not think that they will sing to me.

Modern · 1928

Romance Sonámbulo

Federico García Lorca

Opening — Spanish original (public domain)

Verde que te quiero verde.
Verde viento. Verdes ramas.
El barco sobre la mar
y el caballo en la montaña.
Con la sombra en la cintura
ella sueña en su baranda,
verde carne, pelo verde,
con ojos de fría plata.
Verde que te quiero verde.

A plain English translation, prepared for this curriculum

Green, how I want you green.
Green wind. Green branches.
The ship upon the sea
and the horse on the mountain.
With shadow at her waist
she dreams on her balcony,
green flesh, green hair,
with eyes of cold silver.
Green, how I want you green.

Close Reading

Read, then look closer

Work through these on your own or with a partner. Point to the exact words that make you say what you say.

  • Eliot compares the evening sky to “a patient etherized upon a table.” That’s a strange, cold image for a sunset. What mood does it set for the whole poem?
  • “I have measured out my life with coffee spoons.” What does that line tell you about how Prufrock has spent his time — and how he feels about it?
  • Prufrock keeps almost speaking, then stopping (“Do I dare…?”). What is he so afraid of? Why can he never ask his “overwhelming question”?
  • Read Lorca’s opening aloud in Spanish, even if you don’t speak it. What do you feel from the sound and the repeated word “verde” (green), before you know what it means?
  • Lorca makes wind, branches, flesh, and hair all green. Green isn’t logical here — it’s a feeling. What might all that green stand for: life? sickness? longing? death?

The heart of the pairing

Both poems shatter the tidy story. Eliot fragments the anxious modern mind from the inside; Lorca dissolves reality into the hypnotic color and dream-logic of the surreal. One you follow like a nervous thought; the other you follow like a dream.

How to run the close reading

Teaching poems that resist paraphrase

1. Give students permission not to “solve” them. Both poems reward feeling and attention before full comprehension. Say so up front. The skill this module builds is tolerating difficulty — reading for image, sound, and mood, and letting meaning assemble slowly.

2. Trace Prufrock’s paralysis. Start with the shocking opening simile — a sunset like an anaesthetized patient — and note how it drains the romance out of a “love song.” Then follow the loop of hesitation: “Do I dare?”, life measured in coffee spoons, the fear, the rolled trouser-cuffs, and the final admission that the mermaids “will not sing to me.” The poem enacts a mind too self-conscious to act.

3. Let Lorca’s refrain do its work. Read “Verde que te quiero verde” aloud in Spanish and let the class hear the incantation before translation. Then ask what “green” is doing: it saturates wind, branches, skin, and hair, refusing to settle into one meaning. This is surrealism — image and color carrying emotional truth that plain statement can’t.

4. Name the two kinds of fragmentation. Draw the contrast explicitly. Eliot breaks the intellect: irony, allusion, and stalled logic portray a self that cannot cohere. Lorca breaks logic itself: the poem follows the associative leaps of a dream. Both refuse the smooth Victorian narrative — one because the modern mind is too divided, the other because the deepest reality is the dream.

Eliot’s modernism

The fractured interior: an ironic, allusive, endlessly hesitating consciousness adrift in a shabby modern city. Meaning dissolves into self-doubt.

Lorca’s surrealism

The liberated dream: color, music, and image replace cause and effect. A folk ballad becomes a hypnotic vision you feel more than follow.

Guiding questions · with what to listen for
  1. Why call the poem a “Love Song” when Prufrock never declares any love? Listen for the irony in the title. It promises romance and delivers paralysis — the gap between the grand label and the timid reality is the poem’s subject.
  2. What kind of person “measures out” life in coffee spoons? Listen for a life of tiny, repeated social rituals — safe, trivial, and wasted. The image makes time feel doled out in teaspoon-sized nothings.
  3. In Lorca, does it matter exactly what “green” means? Listen for the recognition that green resonates precisely because it won’t resolve — suggesting youth, nature, sickness, jealousy, death all at once. Surreal images hold several meanings in suspension.
  4. How does the Spanish sound — the repetition, the vowels — shape the poem’s spell? Listen for the ballad’s music: the returning refrain and rolling rhythm make it feel chanted or dreamed, so meaning arrives through the ear.
  5. Both poems leave you without a clear resolution. Is that a failure, or the point? Listen for the payoff: a fractured age produced fractured forms. Eliot’s unfinished question and Lorca’s unexplained dream are honest pictures of minds and worlds that no longer add up neatly.
Annotation prompts · hand these to students

On the Eliot

Circle every question and every “I have” / “Do I dare.” Underline images that feel cold, tired, or mechanical. Where does Prufrock come closest to acting — and what stops him?

On the Lorca

Highlight every appearance of “verde” / green and everything it colors. In the margin, jot the feeling each green thing gives you. Don’t translate the poem into a plot — map its moods instead.

The thesis of the pairing

When the smooth nineteenth-century story broke, poets rebuilt the poem from its pieces. Eliot charts the fractured, ironic interior of a modern mind too anxious to act; Lorca surrenders logic altogether to the color, music, and dream-image of the surreal — the intellect in fragments beside the unconscious set free.

Words & Allusions
Modernism
The early-20th-century movement that broke with traditional form to capture a fractured, disillusioned modern world.
interior monologue
Writing that follows the flow of a character’s private thoughts — the form of “Prufrock.”
allusion
A reference to another text, figure, or artwork (Eliot nods to Michelangelo, Hamlet, and more) that layers extra meaning.
etherized
Put under anaesthetic (ether). Eliot’s numbed, clinical image for the evening sky.
surrealism
An art movement embracing dream-logic, the unconscious, and startling irrational images — the mode of Lorca’s ballad.
romance
Here, the traditional Spanish narrative ballad form Lorca borrows and transforms — not a love story.
refrain
A repeated line — “Verde que te quiero verde” — that gives Lorca’s poem its hypnotic, song-like spell.
Romancero gitano
Lorca’s 1928 book Gypsy Ballads, rooted in the folk culture and landscape of Andalusia in southern Spain.
