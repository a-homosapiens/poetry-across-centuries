III.  The Natural World

The Daffodils and the Mind of Winter

I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud · Wordsworth · 1807 ⟶ The Snow Man · Stevens · 1921

Two poets stand and look at nature — and completely disagree about what the mind should do with what it sees. Wordsworth pours his own joy into a field of daffodils until the flowers seem to dance. Stevens says that’s cheating: to really see the winter, you have to empty yourself of all that feeling first.

So this isn’t “happy nature versus sad nature.” Both scenes could be perfectly neutral. The argument is about us — whether we should paint the world with our feelings, or wipe the glass clean to see it as it actually is.

Teacher’s Edition · History & Background

Pouring the self in, and draining the self out

William Wordsworth was writing at the dawn of the Romantic era. As cities were choked by the Industrial Revolution, he argued that time in open nature was essential to the health of the soul. The daffodils were real: he and his sister Dorothy came upon them by Ullswater in April 1802, and her journal describes them first. But notice what Wordsworth does to them. The flowers become a “crowd,” a “host,” “jocund company”; they “dance,” “toss their heads,” out-do the waves “in glee.” He fills the scene with human feeling — and later, in memory, the scene fills him. This is Romantic imagination at full power: the self and nature merge in a warm circuit of emotion.

A century later, Wallace Stevens — an American modernist who spent his days as an insurance executive in Hartford — wrote the coldest and most beautiful rebuttal imaginable. “The Snow Man” (1921) is a single sentence claiming that to perceive a winter landscape truthfully, “One must have a mind of winter” and “have been cold a long time.” The “misery” we hear in the wind, he says, is not in the wind — we put it there. The ideal observer becomes “nothing himself,” and so beholds “Nothing that is not there and the nothing that is.”

Context worth having ready

The device at the center of this pairing has a name: the pathetic fallacy — giving human emotions to nature (daffodils that feel “glee”). Wordsworth embraces it as the imagination’s glory; Stevens diagnoses it as a kind of lie and strips it away. Stevens’s poem appears in his 1923 collection Harmonium. A useful frame for students: Romanticism trusts the feeling self to complete the world; modernism grows suspicious of that self and wants to see what remains when it is subtracted.

Nature is
Peopled ⟶ Bare

The Poems · Read in Sequence

Classic · 1807

I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud

William Wordsworth

I wandered lonely as a cloud
That floats on high o’er vales and hills,
When all at once I saw a crowd,
A host, of golden daffodils;
Beside the lake, beneath the trees,
Fluttering and dancing in the breeze.

Continuous as the stars that shine
And twinkle on the milky way,
They stretched in never-ending line
Along the margin of a bay:
Ten thousand saw I at a glance,
Tossing their heads in sprightly dance.

The waves beside them danced; but they
Out-did the sparkling waves in glee:
A poet could not but be gay,
In such a jocund company:
I gazed—and gazed—but little thought
What wealth the show to me had brought:

For oft, when on my couch I lie
In vacant or in pensive mood,
They flash upon that inward eye
Which is the bliss of solitude;
And then my heart with pleasure fills,
And dances with the daffodils.

❦ A century later, the mind of winter answers

Modern · 1921

The Snow Man

Wallace Stevens

One must have a mind of winter
To regard the frost and the boughs
Of the pine-trees crusted with snow;

And have been cold a long time
To behold the junipers shagged with ice,
The spruces rough in the distant glitter

Of the January sun; and not to think
Of any misery in the sound of the wind,
In the sound of a few leaves,

Which is the sound of the land
Full of the same wind
That is blowing in the same bare place

For the listener, who listens in the snow,
And, nothing himself, beholds
Nothing that is not there and the nothing that is.

Close Reading

Read, then look closer

Work through these on your own or with a partner. Point to the exact words that make you say what you say.

  • List every word Wordsworth uses that makes the daffodils act like people (start with “crowd,” “dancing,” “glee”). Do flowers really do those things — or is Wordsworth adding something?
  • Stevens says you must “not… think / Of any misery in the sound of the wind.” Where does the “misery” come from, if not the wind?
  • Wordsworth’s poem ends with his heart “danc[ing] with the daffodils.” Stevens’s ends with a listener who is “nothing himself.” Which ending feels true to you, and why?
  • Here’s the real debate: Is Wordsworth painting his own happy mood onto the flowers — a kind of beautiful lie? Or is imagining that nature shares our feelings exactly what makes us human?
  • Both poems are about looking. What does each poet think you should bring to the act of looking — and what should you leave behind?

The heart of the pairing

Wordsworth pours the self into nature until the daffodils dance. Stevens drains the self out until only the snow — and the nothing — remain. Same act of looking; opposite beliefs about the mind.

How to run the close reading

Two theories of what the mind should do with a view

1. Catch Wordsworth in the act of projecting. Have students underline every verb and adjective that hands the daffodils a human inner life: “crowd,” “host,” “dancing,” “glee,” “jocund company,” “tossing their heads,” “sprightly.” Nature here is peopled with the poet’s own delight. Name the device: the pathetic fallacy. For Wordsworth this is no error — it is the imagination completing the world.

2. Watch Stevens refuse to project. “The Snow Man” is one long sentence of conditions for not adding anything: “One must have a mind of winter… and have been cold a long time… and not to think / Of any misery in the sound of the wind.” Trace how the poem keeps subtracting warmth, feeling, and finally the self, until the observer is “nothing himself.”

3. Set the two endings side by side. Wordsworth’s memory fills him — “my heart with pleasure fills, / And dances with the daffodils.” Stevens’s beholder is emptied to the point of vanishing and sees “the nothing that is.” Crucially, Stevens’s nothing is not despair — it is clarity, the world seen without our decoration.

4. Stage the debate (this is the heart of the hour). Put the question to the room: Is Stevens right that Wordsworth’s dancing daffodils are a beautiful lie — the poet painting his own mood onto indifferent flowers? Or is projecting feeling onto the world exactly what poetry, and being human, is for? There is no correct answer; the argument is the learning.

Wordsworth’s claim

The feeling self completes nature. Imagination turns a field into “jocund company,” and memory turns a walk into lifelong “wealth.” Emotion is the point of perception.

Stevens’s counter

The feeling self distorts nature. To see the winter truly you must stop hearing your own misery in the wind and become “a mind of winter” — cold, empty, clear.

Guiding questions · with what to listen for
  1. Do daffodils “dance” and feel “glee”? What is Wordsworth actually describing when he says they do? Listen for the recognition that he is describing his own joy and lending it to the flowers — the pathetic fallacy. Then push: does naming it as projection make the poem less true, or more honest about how the mind works?
  2. Why must Stevens’s observer “have been cold a long time”? Listen for the idea that you can only see winter without self-pity once you’ve stopped resisting it — long exposure burns off the impulse to project warmth or misery. Coldness is a discipline, not a mood.
  3. Stevens’s poem is a single sentence. How does that one long, careful sentence match his argument? Listen for the control: no exclamation, no burst of feeling like Wordsworth’s dashes and “glee” — just a cool, conditional structure that enacts the emptied mind it describes.
  4. Is “the nothing that is” a bleak ending or a clear-eyed one? Listen for both readings. Some hear emptiness and cold; others hear a hard-won honesty — the world finally seen without our comforting projections laid over it.
  5. If you had to send one of these poems out to a friend having a hard week, which would you choose — and what does your choice say about what you want from nature? Listen for students articulating the two functions: Wordsworth offers consolation and stored joy; Stevens offers clarity and the dignity of seeing things as they are. Both are gifts.
Annotation prompts · hand these to students

On the Wordsworth

Box every word that gives the daffodils a human life. Then find the exact stanza where the poem leaves the lakeside and moves indoors into memory (“For oft, when on my couch I lie”). Who owns the daffodils now — nature, or the poet’s mind?

On the Stevens

Underline every word of subtraction and cold (“winter,” “cold,” “bare,” “nothing”). Circle the word “misery” and draw an arrow to where the poem says it really comes from. Read the last line aloud twice — is it frightening, or freeing?

The thesis of the pairing

Both poets stand before a landscape and ask what the mind should do with it. The Romantic answers by pouring the self in until the flowers dance and memory overflows; the modernist answers by draining the self out until only the snow, the wind, and “the nothing that is” remain — not sadness, but the world seen at last without us painted over it.

Words & Allusions
pathetic fallacy
Giving human feelings to nature (e.g. daffodils that feel “glee”). The central device of this pairing — embraced by Wordsworth, refused by Stevens.
projection
Casting your own feelings onto something outside you, so it seems to share them.
Romanticism
The movement, around 1800, that prized emotion, imagination, and nature as a path to truth and healing.
jocund
Cheerful, merry — the daffodils are “jocund company.”
inward eye
The mind’s eye — imagination and memory. Wordsworth’s central idea of seeing again, within.
mind of winter
Stevens’s phrase for a perception stripped of warmth and self — the cold clarity needed to see nature plainly.
Harmonium
Stevens’s first collection (1923), where “The Snow Man” appears.
tercet
A three-line stanza. “The Snow Man” is built from five of them, running on as one sentence.
