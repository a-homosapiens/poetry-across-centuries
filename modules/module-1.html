I.  Mythology Remixed

The Woman Who Waited

The Odyssey · Homer · 8th c. BCE ⟶ Penelope · Parker · 1928

We begin where Western storytelling begins — with Homer’s Odyssey — but from its quiet edge. While Odysseus roams the seas for twenty years, his wife Penelope holds their household together back home in Ithaca, fending off a mob of suitors with nothing but patience and cunning.

Homer admires her — but from the outside, mostly through the mouths of men. Almost three thousand years later, Dorothy Parker lets Penelope speak for herself, and quietly asks a sharp question: if he gets called brave for sailing away, what do we call the one who stayed? That is how this whole course works — each classic poem set beside a modern one that answers it. And keep Penelope in mind: at the very end we return to this same epic for a very different silenced woman, the Siren.

Teacher’s Edition · History & Background

The clever wife, and the last word

In Homer’s Odyssey, while Odysseus is away for two decades, his wife Penelope is besieged in Ithaca by more than a hundred suitors who assume he is dead and demand she remarry. Her famous stratagem: she promises to choose a husband once she has finished weaving a burial shroud for Odysseus’s aged father, Laertes — but each night she secretly unravels the day’s work, stalling for three years until a servant betrays her. She is celebrated for fidelity and cleverness, yet the epic keeps her in the domestic sphere; at one point her own son, Telemachus, tells her that speech is men’s business and sends her back upstairs to her loom. We admire Penelope — but we mostly hear about her from others. In the excerpt below, it is a suitor, Antinous, who mockingly tells the story of her trick.

Dorothy Parker’s “Penelope” (1928) hands the loom-bound wife her own voice at last. Parker — the razor-witted American poet and critic of the Algonquin Round Table — writes a short, cool, deadpan poem in Penelope’s first person. She lays Odysseus’s glamorous voyage (“the silver seas,” “the glittering wave”) beside her own small, endless domestic tasks (sit and rock, answer the door, brew tea, bleach the linen) — and closes on a single devastating line: “They will call him brave.”

Context worth having ready

This opening module introduces the idea that runs through the whole course: re-vision — retelling a myth from the point of view it silenced. The Odyssey frames its women from the outside; here Penelope is admired, yet described (in the excerpt below) by one of the very suitors she is outwitting. Dorothy Parker hands her a microphone. Parker’s irony is the engine: her final line says the world will honour his adventure, and leaves hanging — loudly — the fact that no one will call her anything at all, though her patient endurance may be the braver act. The course deliberately closes by returning to this same epic and doing exactly this for its most famous monster — the Siren (Pairing X).

The Poems · Read in Sequence

Classic · 8th c. BCE

The Odyssey

Homer

Book II — the suitor Antinous mocks Penelope’s weaving trick (excerpt)

Elusive of the bridal day, she gives
Fond hopes to all, and all with hopes deceives.
Did not the sun, through heaven’s wide azure roll’d,
For three long years the royal fraud behold?
While she, laborious in delusion, spread
The spacious loom, and mix’d the various thread:

“Though cold in death Ulysses breathes no more,
Cease yet awhile to urge the bridal hour:
Cease, till to great Laertes I bequeath
A task of grief, his ornaments of death.
Lest when the Fates his royal ashes claim,
The Grecian matrons taint my spotless fame.”

The work she plied; but, studious of delay,
By night reversed the labours of the day.
While thrice the sun his annual journey made,
The conscious lamp the midnight fraud survey’d;
Unheard, unseen, three years her arts prevail;
The fourth her maid unfolds the amazing tale.

Then urged, she perfects her illustrious toils;
A wondrous monument of female wiles!

Translated into verse by Alexander Pope (1725). Pope uses “Ulysses,” the Roman name for Odysseus. · public domain

❦ Nearly three millennia later, the wife speaks

Modern · 1928

Penelope

Dorothy Parker

In the pathway of the sun,
In the footsteps of the breeze,
Where the world and sky are one,
He shall ride the silver seas,
He shall cut the glittering wave.
I shall sit at home, and rock;
Rise, to heed a neighbor’s knock;
Brew my tea, and snip my thread;
Bleach the linen for my bed.
They will call him brave.

From Sunset Gun (1928) · public domain

Close Reading

Read, then look closer

Work through these on your own or with a partner. Point to the exact words that make you say what you say.

  • In Homer, who is telling the story of Penelope’s clever trick — and how do you think he feels about her? Does she ever speak for herself here?
  • Homer calls her weaving trick “a wondrous monument of female wiles.” Is that admiration, an insult, or both?
  • Make two columns: everything Parker’s Odysseus does, and everything Penelope does. What is the difference between the two lists?
  • Parker ends on “They will call him brave.” Who are “they,” and what does the poem leave unsaid about Penelope right after that line?
  • This is the first of two pairings that take a woman from the Odyssey and let a modern poet re-tell her — the other, the Siren, closes the whole course. Why might modern poets keep going back to old myths to do this?

The heart of the pairing

Homer honours Penelope’s cleverness, but from the outside, in a man’s mouth. Parker gives her the first-person last word — and turns the quiet question loose: if sailing away is “brave,” what do we call staying, and waiting, and holding everything together?

How to run the close reading

Two portraits of the same waiting woman

1. Notice who holds the microphone. As in Pairing I, point of view is the lesson. In Homer, Penelope is talked about — and, pointedly, by Antinous, one of the very suitors she is outwitting. Her cleverness reaches us secondhand, filtered through a hostile man’s scorn (“the royal fraud,” “female wiles”). Parker removes the filter: the whole poem is Penelope’s own “I.”

2. Weigh the two kinds of labour. Build the contrast physically. Odysseus’s world is vast and glamorous — sun, breeze, silver seas, the glittering wave. Penelope’s is small and repetitive — rock, answer a knock, brew tea, snip thread, bleach linen. Ask students which list took more years, more endurance, more self-control. The poem never says; it makes you feel it.

3. Land the final line. “They will call him brave” is the whole poem’s hinge. Have students articulate everything it implies without stating: that the world’s admiration flows to the one who leaves; that Penelope’s harder, unglamorous constancy will go unnamed; that “brave” may be exactly the wrong word, or the right word aimed at the wrong person. The irony lives in the silence after the line.

4. Plant a seed for the finale. Tell students the course will end back in this same epic with another silenced woman — the Siren (Pairing X), where a modern poet, Margaret Atwood, does to the monster exactly what Parker does here to the wife. Two re-visions of the Odyssey will bracket the whole year, each exposing what the epic’s point of view left out: the inner life, the resentment, the cost.

Homer’s Penelope

A marvel of loyalty and cunning — but framed by others, admired as a “monument of female wiles,” and kept to her loom. We watch her; we don’t hear her.

Parker’s Penelope

A dry, clear-eyed “I” who counts her own chores against his adventures and names the injustice of who gets called brave — without ever raising her voice.

Guiding questions · with what to listen for
  1. Why does it matter that the suitor Antinous, not Penelope, narrates the weaving trick in Homer? Listen for the recognition that her cleverness is being described by an enemy who resents it — “fraud,” “wiles.” The same act could be told as heroism; the framing controls how we judge her.
  2. Parker gives Odysseus grand, open images and Penelope small, closed ones. What does that contrast argue? Listen for how diction and scale carry the theme: his verbs are sweeping (ride, cut); hers are modest and domestic (rock, brew, snip, bleach). The poem values her labour precisely by refusing to glamorize it.
  3. What is left unsaid after “They will call him brave”? Listen for the implied second half: “…and they will call me nothing.” The poem’s power is in the omission — the reader supplies the injustice.
  4. Is Penelope’s waiting passive, or is it its own kind of heroism? Listen for a real debate. Homer’s ruse shows active cunning; Parker’s chores look passive but demand years of endurance. Students often arrive at: constancy is a quieter courage the culture forgets to honour.
  5. This course ends with another woman from the Odyssey — the Siren, its most famous monster. Based on Penelope, what do you predict a modern poet might do with her? Listen for predictions that a modern poet would give the Siren an inner life and a grievance too — which is exactly what Atwood does in the course’s finale. The Odyssey is full of women framed from the outside, waiting to be re-voiced.
Annotation prompts · hand these to students

On the Homer

Underline every word Antinous uses to judge Penelope (“elusive,” “deceives,” “fraud,” “wiles”). In the margin, rewrite one of those words as an admirer would put it. How much of our view of her depends on the speaker?

On the Parker

Draw a line down the poem separating “his” lines from “her” lines. Circle every verb. Then cover the last line and predict it; uncover it and mark what the single short line does to everything above it.

The thesis of the pairing

The Odyssey preserves Penelope as a paragon of loyal cleverness — but describes her from the outside, even through the mouth of an enemy. Parker returns her the first person and, in ten plain lines, redefines the argument: the epic reserves the word “brave” for the man who sails away, while the woman who stays and endures is left unnamed. Giving the silenced their voice, it turns out, can be as quiet as a single closing line.

Words & Allusions
Penelope
The wife of Odysseus, famed for her faithfulness and cleverness during his twenty-year absence.
Ithaca
Odysseus’s island kingdom and home — where Penelope waits and rules in his absence.
the suitors
The crowd of men who, believing Odysseus dead, camp in his house pressing Penelope to remarry.
Antinous
The most arrogant of the suitors; in this excerpt he sneeringly recounts Penelope’s weaving trick.
shroud
A burial cloth. Penelope claims she must finish weaving one for Laertes before she can remarry.
Laertes
Odysseus’s aged father, for whom the never-finished shroud is supposedly being woven.
Algonquin Round Table
The famous circle of New York writers and wits of the 1920s to which Dorothy Parker belonged.
irony
A gap between what is said and what is meant. “They will call him brave” means far more than it states.
