VIII.  Ruins, Time & Legacy

The Colossus and the Glove

Ozymandias · Shelley · 1818 ⟶ Museum · Szymborska

Two poems about time swallowing everything we build. Shelley shows you a giant, shattered statue of a dead king boasting in an empty desert. Szymborska shows you the same truth in a quieter place: a museum, where a glove has outlived the hand it once fit.

One poem is grand and monumental; the other is small, wry, and almost funny. Watch how they arrive at nearly the same idea from completely different sizes.

Teacher’s Edition · History & Background

From the ruined colossus to the glass display case

Percy Bysshe Shelley wrote “Ozymandias” in 1817 as part of a friendly sonnet-writing competition with a friend. The British Museum had recently acquired a massive, fragmented statue of the Egyptian pharaoh Ramesses II (“Ozymandias” is the Greek form of his name). Shelley used that broken colossus to write a warning about the hubris of grand empires and dictators: the tyrant’s boast survives only as an irony, carved on a pedestal in an empty desert.

Over a century later, Wisława Szymborska — who would go on to win the Nobel Prize in Literature — was writing in Poland in the long aftermath of the Second World War. Instead of the giant ruins of kings, she looked at ordinary objects in glass display cases. “Museum” strips away the grandeur of history and settles on a quiet, faintly absurd fact: a metal plate, a fan, or a dress will outlive the person who used it. Where Shelley warns the mighty, Szymborska implicates everyone — including herself.

Context worth having ready

“Ozymandias” is a sonnet with a famous frame structure: the poet reports what a traveller told him, who in turn quotes the inscription the long-dead king had carved. Note that the sculptor outlives the king — art survives power. Szymborska’s “Museum” comes from her 1962 collection Salt; her signature tone is ironic, understated, and disarmingly domestic — mortality delivered with a wink. Because the English translation of “Museum” is still under copyright, it is described here and linked in full rather than reprinted.

The Poems · Read in Sequence

Classic · 1818

Ozymandias

Percy Bysshe Shelley

I met a traveller from an antique land,
Who said—“Two vast and trunkless legs of stone
Stand in the desert. . . . Near them, on the sand,
Half sunk a shattered visage lies, whose frown,
And wrinkled lip, and sneer of cold command,
Tell that its sculptor well those passions read
Which yet survive, stamped on these lifeless things,
The hand that mocked them, and the heart that fed;
And on the pedestal, these words appear:
My name is Ozymandias, King of Kings;
Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!
Nothing beside remains. Round the decay
Of that colossal Wreck, boundless and bare
The lone and level sands stretch far away.”

Contemporary · 1962

Museum

Wisława Szymborska

In copyright · read the full poem via the link below

What happens in the poem

Szymborska walks us through a museum’s glass cases, cataloguing the ordinary things inside — plates, wedding rings, a fan, swords, a lute, a crown, a glove, a shoe, a hairpin. With dry, deadpan wit she keeps noticing the same uncomfortable joke: these objects have outlived the people who once used them. A crown survives the head that wore it; a glove outlasts the hand; a shoe defeats the foot. Time, she observes, has been quietly won by things. Then, in a sudden turn, she points at herself — still alive, for now — and jokes that even the dress she is wearing is stubbornly determined to go on existing after she is gone. The comedy is how gently she smuggles in our own mortality.

Close Reading

Read, then look closer

Work through these on your own or with a partner. Point to the exact words that make you say what you say.

  • Ozymandias’s inscription says “Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!” What did he mean those words to say — and what do they actually mean now that “Nothing beside remains”?
  • Shelley never speaks to the king directly. The story reaches us through a traveller, who quotes an inscription. Why might the poem hide the king behind so many layers?
  • Szymborska’s museum is full of small, ordinary things — a fan, a lute, a shoe, a glove. Pick one. What has been lost that the object has outlived?
  • Szymborska describes objects as if they’re winning a battle against people. Why is that image both funny and a little frightening?
  • Shelley’s poem is huge and grand; Szymborska’s is small and almost joking. They reach nearly the same idea. Which one unsettles you more, and why?

The heart of the pairing

Both poems watch objects outlast the people who made or owned them. Shelley aims the lesson at kings and empires; Szymborska quietly aims it at you.

How to run the close reading

The same truth, at two opposite scales

1. Unpack Shelley’s irony first. The engine of “Ozymandias” is dramatic irony. The boast — “Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!” — was meant to terrify rivals with the king’s greatness. Time has inverted it: the only thing to despair at is how completely everything has vanished. Have students read the inscription in the king’s intended tone, then reread it in the desert’s actual tone.

2. Count the frames. The poem is a nest of voices: Shelley → the traveller → the inscription → the dead king — and behind them the sculptor “whose hand mocked” those passions. The king’s power is buried under layers of narration, which is itself the point: he no longer speaks for himself. Note the quiet winner — the sculptor’s art has survived the ruler’s power.

3. Switch scales with Szymborska. Where Shelley gives one monumental ruin, “Museum” gives an inventory of tiny things — plates, rings, a fan, a lute, a crown, a glove, a shoe. Read the catalogue’s effect: the accumulation of humble objects does quietly what Shelley’s desert does grandly, and her deadpan rhetorical questions (asking where the vanished people and feelings have gone) sharpen the irony.

4. Find the tonal turn. Szymborska personifies the objects as victors — the crown beating the head, the glove beating the hand — a wry, almost comic conceit. Then the poem turns intimate: she brings in her own living body and her stubborn, doomed dress. The joke suddenly includes the poet herself, and us. The humor is how she smuggles in mortality.

Shelley’s mode

The sublime: a single colossal ruin, “boundless and bare,” a warning aimed upward at “ye Mighty.” Grand, distant, and moral.

Szymborska’s mode

The domestic and ironic: a glass case of small objects, aimed at everyone, including herself. Intimate, funny, and unnervingly close.

Guiding questions · with what to listen for
  1. How does time change the meaning of the words “Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!”? Listen for the reversal: intended as a threat about greatness, it now reads as an accidental truth about emptiness. The irony is produced entirely by the surrounding “lone and level sands.”
  2. Who actually “survives” in Shelley’s poem? Listen for students noticing the sculptor: “the hand that mocked” the king’s passions. The artist’s reading of the tyrant outlasts the tyrant’s power — a sly claim about art versus authority.
  3. Szymborska lists humble objects rather than a grand monument. What does that choice do to the poem’s argument about time? Listen for the democratization of the theme: it’s not only pharaohs who lose to time — a fan, a lute, an ordinary shoe make the point that everyone’s things outlast them. Smaller scale, wider reach.
  4. Why does Szymborska make the objects sound like they’re winning against people? Listen for how the comic personification masks a grim truth: matter is more durable than the living. The humor lowers our defenses so the mortality lands harder when she turns to herself.
  5. Why does Shelley end on empty sand while Szymborska ends on her own dress? Listen for the contrast in closing gestures: Shelley pulls the camera back to vast, impersonal emptiness (the sublime); Szymborska zooms in to her own clothing (personal, mortal, and funny). Same theme, opposite exits.
Annotation prompts · hand these to students

On the Shelley

Mark each “voice” in the poem with a bracket (poet, traveller, inscription). Underline every word of ruin and emptiness in the last four lines (“decay,” “colossal Wreck,” “boundless and bare,” “lone and level sands”). Ask: how do the sounds of those final lines feel empty?

On the Szymborska

Using the summary and the linked full text, list the objects in the museum. Beside each, jot the human thing it has outlived (a hand, a foot, a feeling). Then find the moment the poem stops describing the museum and turns to the poet herself. What changes in tone?

The thesis of the pairing

A century apart, both poets confront the same fact — things outlast the people who made and owned them. Shelley makes it monumental and moral, a warning carved for tyrants; Szymborska makes it miniature and personal, a joke told in a glass case that ends up being about her own body, and ours.

Words & Allusions
Ozymandias
The Greek name for the Egyptian pharaoh Ramesses II, one of ancient Egypt’s most powerful and monument-building kings.
antique land
An ancient country — here, Egypt. “Antique” means old, not “for sale.”
visage
A face. The statue’s “shattered visage” lies half-buried in the sand.
pedestal
The base a statue stands on — where the king’s boastful inscription is carved.
colossal
Enormous. A “colossus” is a giant statue; the ruin is a “colossal Wreck.”
sonnet
A 14-line poem. “Ozymandias” is a sonnet — part of what makes its compression so striking.
irony
A gap between intended and actual meaning — the tyrant’s boast becomes proof of his defeat.
catalogue
A poetic list. Szymborska’s inventory of museum objects builds her argument by accumulation.
