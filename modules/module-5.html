V.  The Cityscape & the New Rhythm

The Chartered Street and the Blues

London · Blake · 1794 ⟶ The Weary Blues · Hughes · 1925

Two poets walk a hard city at night. Blake’s London is locked down — every street “charter’d” (owned and controlled), every face marked by misery, the rhythm as rigid as a chain. A century later, Hughes walks into Harlem and hears something Blake never could: a piano player bending sorrow into the blues.

Listen to how the two poems sound. Blake’s beat never breaks — it feels trapped. Hughes lets the line sway and syncopate like the music itself, and in doing so he changed what Western poetry could sound like.

Teacher’s Edition · History & Background

From the manacled city to the swinging line

William Blake’s “London” (from Songs of Experience, 1794) is a furious, tightly controlled indictment of a city crushed by poverty and institutional power. The speaker walks streets and even a river that are “charter’d” — mapped, owned, licensed — and sees “marks of weakness, marks of woe” on every face. His most famous phrase, “the mind-forg’d manacles,” argues that the chains are mental as much as economic: people have internalized their own oppression. In turn Blake indicts the Church (which lets chimney-sweeps suffer), the monarchy (a soldier’s blood on palace walls), and even marriage (disease passing to newborns).

Langston Hughes’s “The Weary Blues” (1925) is a landmark of the Harlem Renaissance. It recreates a night on Lenox Avenue in Harlem, listening to a Black musician play the blues by lamplight. Hughes does something radical for “serious” poetry: he pours the syncopated rhythm of jazz and blues directly into the verse, and even embeds the singer’s own lyrics inside the poem. The music carries deep weariness and sorrow — but also endurance, artistry, and survival.

Context worth having ready

The pairing is really about rhythm as argument. Blake writes in relentless iambic tetrameter and hammering repetition (“In every… in every…”); the regular beat is the manacle, the form enacting the trap. Hughes breaks that regularity open with the swing, drawl, and repeated refrains of the blues — a distinctly African American musical form — reshaping the poetic line itself. One city clamps down; the other finds, inside its hardship, a new music. (Hughes’s text preserves the spelling and dialect of his 1925 original.)

The Poems · Read in Sequence

Classic · 1794

London

William Blake

I wander thro’ each charter’d street,
Near where the charter’d Thames does flow.
And mark in every face I meet
Marks of weakness, marks of woe.

In every cry of every Man,
In every Infant’s cry of fear,
In every voice: in every ban,
The mind-forg’d manacles I hear.

How the Chimney-sweeper’s cry
Every black’ning Church appalls;
And the hapless Soldier’s sigh
Runs in blood down Palace walls.

But most thro’ midnight streets I hear
How the youthful Harlot’s curse
Blasts the new-born Infant’s tear,
And blights with plagues the Marriage hearse.

Modern · 1925

The Weary Blues

Langston Hughes

Droning a drowsy syncopated tune,
Rocking back and forth to a mellow croon,
I heard a Negro play.
Down on Lenox Avenue the other night
By the pale dull pallor of an old gas light
He did a lazy sway . . .
He did a lazy sway . . .
To the tune o’ those Weary Blues.
With his ebony hands on each ivory key
He made that poor piano moan with melody.
O Blues!
Swaying to and fro on his rickety stool
He played that sad raggy tune like a musical fool.
Sweet Blues!
Coming from a black man’s soul.
O Blues!

In a deep song voice with a melancholy tone
I heard that Negro sing, that old piano moan—
“Ain’t got nobody in all this world,
Ain’t got nobody but ma self.
I’s gwine to quit ma frownin’
And put ma troubles on the shelf.”

Thump, thump, thump, went his foot on the floor.
He played a few chords then he sang some more—
“I got the Weary Blues
And I can’t be satisfied.
Got the Weary Blues
And can’t be satisfied—
I ain’t happy no mo’
And I wish that I had died.”

And far into the night he crooned that tune.
The stars went out and so did the moon.
The singer stopped playing and went to bed
While the Weary Blues echoed through his head.
He slept like a rock or a man that’s dead.

Text as published in 1925, preserving Hughes’s original spelling and dialect.

Close Reading

Read, then look closer

Work through these on your own or with a partner. Point to the exact words that make you say what you say.

  • Read Blake aloud and tap the beat. Does the rhythm ever break or relax? How does that steady beat make the city feel?
  • Blake repeats “charter’d” and “every.” What is the effect of hearing the same words hammered again and again?
  • What are “mind-forg’d manacles”? If the chains are in the mind, who is doing the imprisoning?
  • Now read Hughes aloud. Where does his rhythm sway, stretch, or repeat like a song? Find the lines that are actually the musician’s blues lyrics.
  • Blake’s city offers no relief. Hughes’s musician is weary and sad — yet he makes music. What does the blues do with his suffering that Blake’s London never does?

The heart of the pairing

Blake hammers his London into a rigid, rhyming cage, so the form itself feels imprisoned. Hughes lets the blues bend and syncopate the line — turning sorrow into a music that survives it, and remaking what a poem can sound like.

How to run the close reading

Make students hear the difference first

1. Clap Blake’s meter. Read “London” aloud and have the class tap the four-beat line. It never lets up — regular, marching, inescapable. Then connect the sound to the sense: a poem about being trapped is written in a form that feels trapped. The metrical regularity is itself one of the “mind-forg’d manacles.”

2. Map Blake’s indictment. Each middle stanza names an institution and its victim: Church / chimney-sweep, monarchy / dead soldier, marriage / diseased newborn. Have students label them. Notice the repetition of “every” — the misery is total, admitting no exception, no escape.

3. Find the swing in Hughes. Now read “The Weary Blues” aloud. The rhythm sways (“He did a lazy sway…”), stretches, and repeats like a refrain (“O Blues! … Sweet Blues!”). Point out the embedded song lyrics: the poem literally contains the musician’s blues, blurring the line between poem and music, literature and the vernacular voice.

4. Compare what suffering becomes. This is the pairing’s core. Blake’s pain stays pain — a diagnosis with no cure. Hughes’s pain is real too (“And I wish that I had died”), but it passes through art: the player turns weariness into blues, and at the end finally sleeps. Ask whether the music heals, or merely endures — both are defensible.

Blake’s London

A closed system. Uniform meter and total repetition enact a city where every person, institution, and even the river is chartered and chained.

Hughes’s Harlem

An open, syncopated form. The blues bends the line, admits the vernacular, and finds inside hardship a music that expresses and outlasts it.

Guiding questions · with what to listen for
  1. Why does Blake repeat the word “charter’d” — even applying it to the river Thames? Listen for the outrage that everything, even flowing water, has been claimed, mapped, and owned. Nothing in the city is free; the repetition makes ownership feel suffocating.
  2. Unpack “the mind-forg’d manacles.” Why “mind-forg’d” rather than iron? Listen for the radical claim that oppression is partly internalized — the people help forge their own chains through fear and resignation. That makes the trap deeper than mere laws.
  3. How does Hughes get the sound of music into words on a page? Listen for specifics: repetition and refrains (“O Blues!”), swaying long lines, onomatopoeia (“Thump, thump, thump”), dialect, and the embedded lyrics. Form imitates performance.
  4. The poem ends “He slept like a rock or a man that’s dead.” Is that peace, or exhaustion? Listen for the ambiguity. The music has spent itself and the man; sleep is release but also a little deathlike. The blues gives voice to weariness without pretending to cure it.
  5. Both poems are about hardship in a city. Why does one feel like a cage and the other like a song? Listen for the payoff: Blake’s fixed form encloses the suffering; Hughes’s musical form transforms it. Rhythm is the argument in both.
Annotation prompts · hand these to students

On the Blake

Circle every repeated word (“charter’d,” “every,” “marks”). Underline each institution he blames. Mark the beat of one stanza with slashes — does the rhythm ever get to rest?

On the Hughes

Highlight every line that is actually the musician’s song. Mark where the rhythm sways or repeats. Then note the last line — what has the long night of music finally done to the player?

The thesis of the pairing

A century apart, two poets walk a hard city after dark. Blake forges his London into rigid, rhyming manacles so the form itself feels imprisoned; Hughes lets the blues syncopate and bend the line, turning suffering into a music that endures it — and, in the bargain, opening Western poetry to the rhythms of jazz and the vernacular voice.

Words & Allusions
charter’d
Licensed, mapped, and owned. Blake’s bitter word for a city where even the river is claimed as property.
mind-forg’d manacles
Blake’s famous phrase for chains made in the mind — oppression that people internalize and help create.
Songs of Experience
Blake’s 1794 collection (paired with Songs of Innocence) exposing the injustices of the adult, industrial world.
Marriage hearse
Blake’s shocking oxymoron — marriage joined to death — as disease passes to the newborn.
Harlem Renaissance
The flowering of African American art, music, and writing centered in Harlem in the 1920s — Hughes’s world.
the blues
An African American musical form built on repetition and a call of sorrow and endurance — the shape of Hughes’s poem.
syncopation
A rhythm that stresses the offbeat, giving jazz and blues their swing — which Hughes carries into the poetic line.
vernacular
Everyday spoken language and dialect. Hughes brings it into “serious” poetry as a source of music and truth.
